Moving an animal out of Panama is not hard because of the flight. It is hard because of the
calendar: a chain of documents with expiry windows that have to line up with each other and with
the day of travel. If one link is done outside its window, it has to be redone, and sometimes
that means losing the flight.

## It is built backwards

The right way to plan it is to start from the flight date and **work back**:

- **Months ahead.** The strictest destinations (European Union, United Kingdom, Australia, Japan)
  require laboratory tests with long lead times, and sometimes a mandatory waiting period between
  the test and travel. There is no shortcut here: if you did not start in time, the date moves.
- **Weeks ahead.** Buy the crate and **let the animal sleep in it**. A dog entering its crate for
  the first time at the airport travels far worse than one that already treats it as its own.
- **Days ahead.** The veterinary health certificate, which nearly always has to be issued within a
  short window before the flight, and the export health permit.
- **On the day.** Arrive with margin. Animals are checked in at a different counter and earlier
  than baggage.

That calendar is the actual work. The rest is executing it.

## The crate is where most bookings fall over

Airlines refuse any crate at check-in that does not meet the **IATA** standard, and the criteria
are concrete and measurable: the animal must be able to stand without lowering its head, turn
around, and lie down in a natural position.

An undersized crate is the most common reason a booking is refused on the day of the flight, and
it is entirely avoidable: you measure the animal and buy the right size. That is why we do it
rather than leaving it to the pet shop's judgement.

Other details also checked at the counter: metal door, bolts rather than plastic clips,
ventilation on at least three sides, a water bowl reachable from outside, and wheels removed if
it has them.

## Breeds and temperature, two filters that do not bend

**Flat-faced breeds** (bulldog, pug, boxer, Persian and others) have short airways and struggle
in flight. Many airlines refuse them in the hold, and some accept them only in cabin if size
allows.

**Temperature.** Nearly all airlines cancel live-animal bookings if temperatures above or below
certain limits are forecast at **any** stop on the route, not just origin and destination. This
shapes the route and sometimes the time of year.

Both are knowable at planning stage. Finding out at the counter is the worst way to discover
them.

## Cabin, hold or cargo

Three different things, worth not confusing:

- **Cabin.** Only if animal and carrier stay under the airline's weight limit, which is usually
  low. It goes under the seat in front.
- **Hold as checked baggage.** Same aircraft as you, in a pressurised, temperature-controlled
  area. This is standard for medium and large dogs.
- **Cargo.** The animal travels as a shipment, not tied to your ticket. That is what applies when
  the airline does not take pets on your flight, when the destination requires it, or when the
  animal travels without you.

The hold sounds worse than it is: it is a conditioned compartment, not a boot.

## And at destination

The process does not end on landing. Each country has its own entry procedure, and some
destinations require quarantine, which can run from hours to weeks depending on origin and
paperwork. That is knowable before you buy the ticket, not after.

## If you have a bird or an unusual pet

The process changes. Beyond the health permit, **CITES** rules may apply if the species is
protected, and some airlines simply will not carry them. It can be done, but it has to be studied
case by case and started with considerably more time.
