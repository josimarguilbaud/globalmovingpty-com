It is the question that comes up most during the home survey, usually halfway through the
kitchen: **"can I take this?"**. The honest answer is rarely yes or no, because it depends on
three separate things that people tend to collapse into one.

## Three things to check, and they are not the same

**Voltage.** Panama runs on 110-120V. So do the United States, Canada, Mexico, Colombia and
much of Central America. Europe, the United Kingdom, Australia and most of Asia run on
220-240V. A 110V appliance plugged into 220V burns out, usually within seconds and for good.

**Frequency.** This is the one almost nobody checks and the one that ruins most appliances.
Panama runs at 60 Hz; Europe at 50 Hz. A transformer converts voltage but **does not change
frequency**. Anything with a motor (fridge, washer, dryer, blender, air conditioning) turns
slower at 50 Hz, runs hot and wears out early. It may work for a while and die within a year,
which is the worst of both worlds because by then you have already paid the freight.

**The plug.** The only one of the three that five dollars solves. A plug adapter converts
nothing: it just lets the appliance fit the wall. If the voltage is wrong, the adapter simply
lets you burn it more comfortably.

## What travels well and what does not

Most modern electronics are **dual voltage**: laptops, phone chargers, consoles, routers,
cameras. You can tell from the label or the power supply. If it says 100-240V and 50/60Hz, it
travels anywhere and only needs a plug adapter.

| Usually worth shipping | Usually not worth it |
|---|---|
| Laptops, tablets, phones and their chargers | Fridge and freezer |
| Recent consoles and audio equipment | Washer and dryer |
| Cordless tools (the charger is what travels) | Microwave |
| Recent televisions, after checking the label | Air conditioning |
| Small appliances without motors, if dual voltage | Vacuum cleaners and motor blenders |

The practical rule: **anything with a motor or a large heating element is better sold here**.
Consumer electronics almost always travel.

## The transformer is not the fix it looks like

Transformers that step 220V down to 110V exist, and they work. The problem is the whole
package: one rated to run a fridge is heavy, expensive, draws power on its own and has to stay
switched on permanently. And it still does not fix the frequency.

For one small, low-draw appliance it makes sense. To run a whole house, it costs more than
buying the appliances at destination, with a worse result.

## The sum worth doing first

Freight is charged **by volume**, not by value. A large fridge takes up roughly a cubic metre
and a half of the container. Before shipping it, the useful question is:

1. What does that volume cost in my quote?
2. What does an equivalent new one cost at destination?
3. What can I sell this one for here?

When (1) plus (3) approaches (2), shipping it stops making economic sense, and that happens
more often than people expect. At the survey we tell you what each item takes up, which is the
number missing from that sum.

## What you should always take

The things with no equivalent: whatever carries sentimental value, whatever was made to
measure, whatever you cannot buy there. An appliance is replaceable in any country; the piece
you inherited is not. When container space is tight, that is the right order of priority.

## A word on gas appliances

Gas cookers are calibrated for a specific gas type, and not every country uses the same one.
Many accept a conversion kit and some do not. And mind the obvious: **the gas cylinder does not
travel**, full or empty. It is dangerous goods and it does not go in a move.
