"How long does it take?" is the second question on every quote, right after the price. And the
usual answer, sailing time, is the one that matters least, because it is only one of the five
legs you have to add up.

## The five legs

**1. Preparation and packing.** Days, not hours. A two-bedroom apartment packs in a day; a large
house with china, a library and paintings, in two or three. It happens before loading day, not
on the same day.

**2. Waiting for space.** The container does not leave when you finish packing, it leaves when
there is a vessel. In peak season space fills up, and that is where a date booked early is worth
money. If you are in a **shared container**, you also wait for it to fill with other cargo.

**3. Sailing and transshipments.** This is the leg people think is the total. Almost no route
from Panama to Europe is direct: there is a transshipment at an intermediate port, and that
change of vessel adds days that do not appear in the transit time the shipping line advertises.

**4. Clearance at destination.** The cargo arrives and is not delivered the same day. The
declaration has to be filed, release has to come through, and if customs decides to inspect,
opening and repacking have to be arranged.

**5. Final delivery.** Port to your home, with unloading, furniture reassembly and unpacking if
you booked it.

## Why we give windows, not dates

Because three of those five legs are outside anyone's control: space depends on the shipping
line, transshipment depends on the intermediate port, and inspection depends on the authority at
destination.

Anyone giving you an exact delivery date before the cargo sails is giving you a number that
sounds better and works worse. What can be done is give you a realistic window at quoting and
tell you once the cargo is loaded aboard, which is when the estimate becomes reliable.

## Air against sea

Air freight cuts legs 2 and 3 dramatically and multiplies the cost. It makes sense for what you
need on arrival, not for the whole house.

The combination that works in practice: **the essentials by air, the rest by sea**. Seasonal
clothes, papers, work equipment and the children's school things fly; the furniture waits. We
suggest it whenever your arrival date and your shipment's do not line up, because the
alternative is six weeks sleeping in an empty house.

## What you can do to gain time

- **Fix an approximate date early.** An estimated date is enough to hold space; the exact day is
  adjusted later.
- **Have the paperwork ready before sailing.** Clearance is prepared while the cargo is still
  here. A missing document once the container has arrived costs days of storage.
- **Decide on storage in advance.** If your home at destination is not ready, storing the goods
  is cheaper than forcing a handover date or missing the sailing.
- **Do not leave unpacking as an afterthought.** If you book it, it is coordinated with delivery
  and no second visit is needed.

## And if something runs late

You get told. A transshipment slipping by a week is not a service failure, it is how ocean
freight works. What would be a failure is you finding out on your own, calling to ask where your
house is.
